What is a Schedule Variance Calculator?

A Schedule Variance (SV) calculator is a project management tool that uses the Earned Value Management (EVM) methodology — defined in the PMI PMBOK® Guide — to measure how far ahead or behind schedule a project is at any given point. It computes two key EVM indicators: Schedule Variance (SV) and Schedule Performance Index (SPI).

Schedule Variance expresses the difference between work completed (Earned Value) and work that should have been completed by now (Planned Value) in monetary terms. A positive SV means more work has been done than planned; a negative SV means the project is running behind. The SPI expresses this as a ratio — an SPI of 0.9 means you are completing only 90 cents of work for every $1 of work that should have been done.

Why Should You Use the Schedule Variance Calculator?

  • Objective schedule health measurement: Removes subjectivity from schedule assessments — SV and SPI give a precise, data-driven answer to "are we on track?"
  • Early warning system: An SPI below 1.0 is an early signal that schedule recovery actions are needed before the project reaches a critical delay.
  • Stakeholder communication: A simple currency figure (e.g., "We are $10,000 worth of work behind schedule") communicates schedule status in a way that non-technical stakeholders instantly understand.
  • Forecasting future completion: SPI feeds into Estimate at Completion (EAC) and Time Estimate at Completion (TEAC) formulas for reliable project end-date forecasting.
  • PMI/PMBOK compliance: EVM is the standard approach for schedule measurement in government contracts, construction, and enterprise software projects.
  • Cross-project comparison: SPI is a normalized ratio, so it can be used to compare schedule performance across projects of different sizes or budgets.

How to Use the Schedule Variance Calculator

  1. Select your currency — the currency in which your project budget is denominated.
  2. Enter Earned Value (EV) — the budgeted cost of work actually completed. EV = % Complete × Total Budget (BAC). For example, if your project is 45% complete on a $100,000 budget, EV = $45,000.
  3. Enter Planned Value (PV) — the amount of budget that was supposed to be spent on work by today, according to your baseline schedule. If the plan says 50% should be done by now, PV = $50,000.
  4. Click Calculate. Schedule Variance, SPI, and variance percentage appear instantly.
  5. Download the CSV or print the result for project status reports or client presentations.

How to Calculate Schedule Variance (SV) and SPI

Schedule Variance (SV) = Earned Value (EV) − Planned Value (PV)
Schedule Performance Index (SPI) = EV ÷ PV
Variance Percentage (SV%) = (SV ÷ PV) × 100

Worked Example

Project baseline: $100,000 total budget. At this point in the schedule, 50% of work should have been done (PV = $50,000). Actual completion: 45% (EV = $45,000).

  • SV = $45,000 − $50,000 = −$5,000 🔴 Behind Schedule
  • SPI = $45,000 ÷ $50,000 = 0.90 (only 90% of planned work done per $1 spent)
  • SV% = (−$5,000 ÷ $50,000) × 100 = −10%

Pros and Cons

✅ Advantages⚠️ Limitations
PMI-standard EVM metric — recognized internationally across government and enterprise projects Requires an accurate baseline plan and regular % complete tracking to produce meaningful results
SPI is a normalized ratio, enabling meaningful cross-project comparison SV naturally trends toward zero as the project approaches 100% complete, regardless of how delayed it was
Expresses schedule status in financial terms — accessible to non-technical stakeholders Does not identify which specific tasks or milestones are causing the delay
Feeds directly into Estimate at Completion (EAC) and forecast completion date calculations Does not account for the critical path — a task may be late but not on the critical path, so it may not impact the end date
Complements Cost Variance (CV) for a complete project health picture Quality of the result depends entirely on the accuracy of EV measurement — "% complete" estimates can be subjective

What Your SPI Could Be Telling You

SPI > 1.1 — Significantly Ahead

Excellent schedule performance. Verify the % complete figures are accurate and not overestimated. Consider whether resources can be reallocated to other projects.

check_circle

SPI 0.95–1.1 — On Track

Within acceptable tolerance. Continue monitoring. Minor course corrections may be needed to maintain this position through the remaining work.

warning

SPI 0.8–0.95 — Slight Delay

Schedule is slipping. Review the critical path and identify specific delayed tasks. Consider adding resources or reducing scope before the situation worsens.

trending_down

SPI Below 0.8 — Formal Recovery Needed

A serious delay. A formal schedule recovery plan is required. Escalate to senior stakeholders and reassess the project end date.

Track SV Alongside CPI

Cost Variance (CV = EV − AC) tells you whether you are over or under budget. Use both SPI and CPI together for a complete EVM project health assessment.

functions

Forecast Using SPI

Use SPI to forecast your completion date: TEAC = Original Duration ÷ SPI. If the project is 120 days long and SPI = 0.9, estimated completion = 120 ÷ 0.9 = 133 days.
